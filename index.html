Why tag your songs?

For God's Glory and our Good

When the church sings, we sing to God, bringing Glory to His name, and in doing so we sing for the benefit of each other. Congregational worship does both.

5

Vertical

Sung to God

Songs and expressions that ascribe worth to God. Their direction is upward, and they're often a response to the revelation of Jesus. These songs echo what heaven is already doing.

For example: Holy, Holy, Holy · Great Are You, Lord · Holy Forever

1

Horizontal

Sung about God, edifying one another

Songs about God, His Word, and His works. They're declarative: they proclaim truth and doctrine, and they edify the body as we sing that truth together.

For example: Christ Our Hope in Life and Death · Is He Worthy?

Which should we choose? Both.

The right balance isn't always the middle. When the moment calls for ascribing glory to God, we lean vertical. When the body needs to be reminded of eternal truth, we lean horizontal.

Either way, singing together is never only personal. Scripture says we address one another as we sing to the Lord (Ephesians 5:19–21), and that everything we do when we gather is for edifying one another (1 Corinthians 14:26). Greg Gilbert of 9Marks applies this to singing: sing even the songs that aren't your favorites, and sing them wholeheartedly, because someone beside you may be deeply strengthened by them. That's true whether you're on a microphone, holding a guitar, or running slides from the back.

From revelation to response

Worship often moves from revelation to response. Many churches shape a service to retell the gospel, and that flow tends to move from horizontal toward vertical:

  1. God Who He is: His nature and attributes
  2. Us Our sin, our condition, and our need
  3. Christ His atoning work: our assurance and salvation
  4. Response How we answer all of it

Where tagging helps

A direction doesn't rank a song. A 1 isn't better or worse than a 5. It simply names which way the song speaks, so your team can:

  • plan a service's flow from revelation to response on purpose, not by feel alone
  • notice when a season, a series, or your most-used songs lean heavily one way
  • filter and report on it in Planning Center, because the directions are ordinary song tags
  • talk about songs with shared language across musicians, tech, and leadership

Planning tools have their limits. Whatever we do, in word or deed, we do in the name of the Lord Jesus, giving thanks to the Father through Him (Colossians 3:17). Good intentions and careful planning don't make our worship acceptable; as Harold Best reminds us, only God, seeing faithfully offered work as perfect in Christ, does that.

Further reading: Harold M. Best, Unceasing Worship · Greg Gilbert on congregational singing at 9Marks

Built to be safe

Your library stays intact.

What the tool does

  • Changes only the 1–5 direction tag on each song. Lists, themes, seasons, and every other tag are kept.
  • Sends 3 songs first and waits until you've checked them in Planning Center.
  • Is safe to run again: songs that already have the right direction are skipped.
  • Keeps a record of every song it sends in a log file.
  • Includes a practice mode with made-up songs that changes nothing.

What stays with you

  • Your Planning Center key is saved only on your computer, and you can turn it off anytime in Planning Center.
  • Song exports and lyrics stay on your computer and are never included in the project files.
  • Claude reads lyrics only to choose a direction and is instructed never to reproduce them.
  • The code is open source, so anyone can read exactly what it does.

Questions

Frequently asked

Is a vertical song better than a horizontal one?

No. Both directions are biblical, and healthy corporate worship needs both. A direction describes how a song speaks, not its quality or its worth. A church may lean one way for a season, and that's fine. The point is to choose on purpose.

Do I need to know how to code?

No. You double-click a file, choose numbers from a menu, and fill in a spreadsheet. The Admin Guide is written for office admins and volunteers, and covers installing Python (a free program the tool needs) on Mac and Windows.

Will it change anything else in Planning Center?

No. It changes only the 1–5 direction tag on each song and keeps every other tag. It also sends 3 songs first so you can check them before it does the rest.

Do I need Claude or any other AI?

No. You can tag every song yourself with the printed rubric. Claude is optional and makes the job faster: it suggests a direction for every song and flags only the songs it's unsure about for you to review.

What does it cost?

The tool is free and open source. You need a Planning Center Services account that can edit songs. If you choose to use Claude, the usual Claude plan applies.

What happens to our song lyrics?

Lyrics come from your own Planning Center export and stay on your computer. They're shared with Claude only if you choose that route, and Claude is instructed to use them only to choose a direction, never to reproduce them. The project files never include any lyrics.

Can we use different labels or a different scale?

Yes. Planning Center's tag names can say anything as long as each starts with its number, 1 through 5. You can also adapt the rubric to your context. Just make sure everyone on your team uses the same one.

What about songs we add later?

Export again and choose to leave out songs that are already tagged. Your new tagging sheet will include only the new songs.

Is this made by Planning Center?

No. It's an independent tool built by a church worship team and shared for others to use. It uses Planning Center's official API.

Ready to try it?

Start with the practice mode. It runs the whole process with made-up songs and changes nothing in Planning Center.
